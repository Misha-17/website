Aligning explanations with human values: context-sensitive trust calibration in AI decision systems

Shivansh Singh; Dominik Siemon

Behaviour & Information Technology

This study examines how the moral reasoning behind an AI decision influences people’s trust and willingness to agree with it. In a controlled experiment with 160 participants, people evaluated AI decisions before and after seeing explanations grounded in utilitarian, deontological, or virtue-based reasoning. The scenarios covered everyday, corporate, environmental, and medical decisions.

Explanations increased trust and agreement overall, but their effects depended on the setting: deontological reasoning produced the strongest gains in higher-stakes contexts, while utilitarian reasoning had weaker effects in medical scenarios. Mediation analysis suggested that perceived transparency accounted for part of the increase in trust. The paper develops a framework for adapting explanations to the values, expectations, and stakes of a decision context, placing the relationship between explanation style and human judgment at the centre of explainable AI design.

Provisional, Contextual, and Verified: How Professionals Navigate Trust-Control Boundaries with AI

Shivansh Singh; Kirsimarja Blomqvist

Proceedings of the 59th Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (HICSS)

This study investigates how professionals decide when to delegate to AI and when to retain or recover control. Using critical incident analysis with 29 AI users across seven professional domains, it identifies four connected practices: granting AI provisional authority that can be withdrawn, switching control as circumstances change, repeatedly checking outputs, and revising expectations as understanding of the system develops.

Together, these practices show how people actively negotiate trust through their work. A central finding is a control paradox: users need to feel that they retain control before they can trust an AI system enough to delegate to it. The paper connects theories of trust calibration and variable autonomy with concrete professional behaviour, offering design implications for systems that support flexible delegation, verification, and meaningful human oversight as tasks and users’ expectations evolve.

Design Elements for Brand Representation in Conversational Agents

Essi Aittamaa; Dominik Siemon; Shivansh Singh

Proceedings of the 59th Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (HICSS)

How can a conversational agent express a company’s brand through its design? This paper approaches that question using the Computers as Social Actors (CASA) perspective and brand personality theory. A systematic review of 133 papers organizes design elements into 12 categories that shape how people perceive conversational agents, including the agent’s personality, gender, and role.

The contribution connects research on human–agent interaction with the practical task of representing a brand through an interactive system. By bringing these findings together, the paper provides a basis for choosing agent characteristics and behaviours that align with brand values. The categories organize existing design knowledge and offer a starting point for exploring which combinations work in specific organizational contexts.

MASTER’S THESISPublished thesis

Internal User Representations in Large Language Models: From Model Internals to Felt Understanding

Shivansh Singh

LUT University · Master’s thesis · LUTPub

How closely does what a language model encodes about a user relate to what the user actually experiences? My master’s thesis connects mechanistic interpretability with human–AI interaction across five empirical studies. It combines persona probing and activation steering, cross-model analyses of suppression and refusal, a 200-participant personalization experiment, causal-reasoning evaluation on CLadder, and analysis of representation changes during conversational redirection.

The studies document several separations between readable information, generated behaviour, and human experience. Persona labels were detectable without reliable activation steering, while Big Five prompt personalization did not improve measured felt understanding in the tested experiment. On 798 anticommonsense causal questions, an internal probe outperformed generated answers by 14.9 percentage points. Together, the studies offer an empirical account of where these connections break down and show why internal representations, observable behaviour, and user outcomes need to be evaluated separately.

Readable but Not Writable: The Probe-Control Gap in Persona-Steered Language Models

S. Singh; D. Siemon; T. Vreede

Research manuscript

If a persona can be identified inside a language model, can the same information be used to change its behaviour? This study tests that assumption across four instruction-tuned model families and 14 personas. It combines linear probing, additive activation steering, layer-by-layer vocabulary analysis, and geometric tracing of how an injected direction changes as it travels through a network.

Persona probes reached a mean F1 of 0.88, but the headline activation-steering score was 1.63 out of 5 against a baseline of 1.54, without a statistically significant improvement. Prompt steering reached 3.91. The analysis identifies two recurring failure patterns: a mid-network increase in persona-related vocabulary probabilities that collapses before output, and a rotation of injected directions associated with opposing MLP contributions. Additional controls test alternative intervention locations and mechanisms. The contribution is a mechanistic account of why a readable representation may fail as a control handle, scoped to the additive interventions evaluated.

Emotional Productivity: Exploring the Impact of AI Interactions on Employee Well-Being and Workplace Efficiency

Shivansh Singh; Dominik Siemon; Danil Vodolazskii

Proceedings of the 58th Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (HICSS)

This study explores how workplace interactions with AI relate to employees’ emotional experiences and productivity. It uses regression and cluster analyses to examine connections between AI interactions, emotional responses, and work outcomes. One finding complicates a straightforward association between feeling positive and working effectively: emotions such as sadness and stress can be positively associated with productivity.

This points to a tension between performance and emotional well-being that organisations need to consider when introducing AI. The paper examines the ethical implications of these relationships and argues for guidelines that balance efficiency with employees’ psychological needs. Its contribution is to bring emotional experience into the evaluation of workplace AI and to motivate tailored interactions that account for both productivity and employee well-being.

BACHELOR’S THESISRepository thesis

Deep learning-based electricity price forecasting in Finnish energy market

Shivansh Singh

LUT University · Bachelor’s thesis · Electrical Engineering

My bachelor’s thesis investigates how deep learning can improve 24-hour electricity price forecasting in the Finnish energy market. It develops a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BLSTM) model using historical Nord Pool market data for training and evaluation. Forecasting performance is compared with ARIMA and GARCH statistical models and a Deep Neural Network (DNN), using Mean Absolute Error, Root Mean Squared Error, and Mean Absolute Percentage Error.

The reported results show the BLSTM model outperforming ARIMA and GARCH, particularly in representing nonlinear market behaviour and volatile price movements. The work contributes an applied comparison of forecasting approaches in the Finnish market and examines the potential of recurrent neural networks for short-term energy forecasting. It connects model evaluation with practical questions about planning and risk management for electricity market participants.

Innovative Convolutional Neural Network Hybrid for Brain Tumor Segmentation

Isselmou Abd El Kader; Jingfei He; Guizhi Xu; Danil Vodolazskii; Shivansh Singh; Rujun Li; Yunge Duan

WSEAS Transactions on Computers · Volume 23 · pp. 252–261

Accurately separating tumour tissue from brain MRI scans is an important step in image analysis for diagnosis and treatment planning. This study investigates a fully automated segmentation approach that combines convolutional neural networks with fuzzy c-means clustering. The resulting CNN–FCM model brings learned image features together with clustering to address segmentation accuracy and variation in input image dimensions.

The approach is evaluated on the BRATS 2017 dataset using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, Dice coefficient, and recall. The paper reports 96.5% accuracy and an overall Dice coefficient of 93.7%, supporting the feasibility of this hybrid approach within the study’s experimental setting. Its contribution is a model and benchmark evaluation for automated tumour segmentation, providing a basis for further investigation of hybrid deep learning and clustering methods in medical image analysis.

Comprehensive Differential and Analysis of Electromagnetic Waves in Biomedical Engineering: Focus on Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

I. Abd El Kader; J. He; Y. Wang; A. M. Rahman; D. Vodolazskii; S. Singh

Research record · Biomedical engineering

This early biomedical-engineering research focuses on electromagnetic waves and their application to magnetic resonance imaging. It connects my electrical-engineering background with an imaging technology that turns physical signals into information about the body.

The work sits alongside my early research on brain-tumour segmentation and other healthcare applications of AI. Its place in my research trajectory is the connection between the underlying technical mechanism and the information a person can use: a theme that later became central to my work on model internals, interpretation, and human–AI interaction.
